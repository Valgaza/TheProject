# The Lighthouse at Kestrel Point

## The keeper

Maren Ashcroft took the keeper's post at Kestrel Point in the spring of 1904,
when she was twenty-six. The Northern Lights Board had never appointed a woman
to a solitary station before, and the clerk who processed her papers wrote
"irregular" in the margin twice. She kept the appointment letter in a tin under
her bed for thirty-one years.

Kestrel Point sits at the northern tip of the Isle of Bracken, a granite spur
that runs two miles into the Ardmuir Strait. There is no harbour. Supplies came
by the tender *Solan*, which called on the first Tuesday of the month and, in
bad winters, did not call at all. Maren once went eleven weeks without seeing
another person.

She was not lonely in the way people assumed. She kept a lurcher called
Pellinore, who was frightened of gulls and slept on the third landing because
it was warmest. She read whatever the *Solan* brought. She wrote 4,000 pages of
weather notes that are now held, unread by almost anyone, in the Bracken
Maritime Archive.

## The light

The Thornwick Light was built in 1847 by the engineer Callum Roe, who also
built the breakwater at Dunmarrow and died before either was finished. The
tower is 34 metres from base to gallery. There are 197 steps to the lamp room,
and Maren climbed them four times a night in winter.

The lamp itself was a third-order Fresnel lens, ground in Birmingham and
shipped north in fourteen crates. It floated on a bath of mercury, which meant
that a keeper could turn the entire two-tonne assembly with one hand. Its
character was two white flashes every fifteen seconds, visible for fourteen
nautical miles in clear air.

The mercury bath is why keepers at Thornwick were rotated off the station every
seven years. Maren refused the rotation three times. When the Board finally
insisted, in 1935, she had served thirty-one years, which remains the longest
unbroken posting at any Scottish light.

## The night of the Camellia Rose

On 4 November 1911 the barque *Camellia Rose* came onto the Skerries in a
south-westerly gale, three miles from the tower. She was carrying jute from
Calcutta to Dundee, with a crew of thirty-one.

Maren saw her strike at ten past two in the morning. There was no lifeboat
station on Bracken and no telegraph. What she had was the light.

She did something the Board's regulations did not permit and, for some years
afterwards, pretended she had not done. She stopped the rotation of the lens
and worked the lamp by hand as a signal, occulting it with a wetted canvas
screen — three long, three short, three long, over and over, aimed south along
the strait toward the fishing village of Corrieholm.

The Corrieholm boats put out at twenty to four. They took twenty-three men off
the Skerries before the tide turned. Eight were lost, including the master,
whose name was Aleister Fenn.

The Board's inquiry criticised Maren for interrupting the light's character —
a passing vessel relying on two-flashes-every-fifteen would have been
misled — and then, in the same paragraph, recommended her for the Board's
silver medal. She was awarded it in March 1912. She never wore it. It was found
in the tin with the appointment letter.

## Pellinore

The dog died in 1919 and is buried under the cairn on the western slope, about
sixty paces from the tower door. Maren cut his name into the capstone with a
cold chisel over four evenings. The lettering is still legible, though the
second L has weathered badly and is often misread as an I.

She never kept another dog. In a letter to her sister Ellen, dated 12 January
1920, she wrote that the station felt "correctly empty" now, and that she had
stopped leaving the third landing door open.

## After

Maren Ashcroft left Kestrel Point in October 1935 and moved to a cottage in
Corrieholm, eleven miles down the coast, from which the Thornwick Light was
visible on a clear night. She lived there until 1951. Neighbours reported that
she went outside at dusk and stood watching until the light came on, then went
back in.

Her successor was Tobias Vane, who lasted nineteen months. He wrote in the
station log that the silence at Kestrel Point was "not an absence of sound but
a positive thing, and it has weight." He resigned in May 1937 and became a
schoolmaster in Perth.

The Thornwick Light was automated in 1962. The mercury bath was drained and the
Fresnel lens removed to the Bracken Maritime Archive, where it stands in the
entrance hall with a sign that does not mention Maren Ashcroft. The tower now
runs on a sealed LED array with a range of eighteen nautical miles and a
character of two white flashes every fifteen seconds, unchanged since 1847.

The 197 steps are still there. The Archive runs tours on the second Saturday of
the month, June through September, and the guides tell the story of the
*Camellia Rose* slightly wrong — they usually say she saved all thirty-one.
